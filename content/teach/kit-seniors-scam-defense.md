**Audience:** older adults, caregivers, senior center staff. **Length:** 45 to 60 minutes. **You'll need:** large-print handouts, a phone for demos, and the [home checklist](../printables/home-spark-checklist.md) printed big.

**Tone:** respectful, never condescending. Many people in the room have spotted more scams than the presenter has. Let them share.

## Objectives

By the end, participants can:

1. Name the most common scams aimed at older adults (impostor calls, grandparent emergencies, tech support pop-ups, romance, prizes and lotteries).
2. Use the hang up, look up, call back habit.
3. Set up a family code word.
4. Know who to call if money was lost.

## Hook (5 min)

Ask: *"Who's gotten a call from 'Medicare', 'Social Security' or 'the bank' this year?"* Invite one or two stories. *"Every one of those callers was counting on one thing: that you'd act before you had time to think. Today is about taking that time back."*

## Explanation (15 min)

1. **The scams** (8 min): grandparent and voice clones, government impostors, tech support pop-ups, romance, prize and lottery. They're all different stories with the same four pressures ([Learn the scam reflex, pause and verify](../practices/scams-and-social-engineering/scam-pause-and-verify.md)).
2. **The habit** (4 min): hang up, look up the real number yourself, call back. Real agencies never demand gift cards, wires or crypto.
3. **Protecting money and identity** (3 min): credit freezes and bank alerts ([Freeze your credit (and your kids' credit)](../practices/privacy-and-footprint/priv-credit-freeze.md)).

## Interactive (10 min)

Read three scam scripts aloud. The room calls out "pressure!" each time they hear urgency, secrecy, authority or a strange payment.

## Worked example (5 min)

Show a fake "your computer is infected, call this number" pop-up (a screenshot). Demonstrate closing the browser instead of calling.

## Practice (10 min)

Pairs practice the code-word conversation: "If anyone ever calls saying they're me and in trouble, they'll know our word" ([Agree on a family code word for emergencies](../practices/scams-and-social-engineering/scam-family-code-word.md)).

## Misconceptions to address

- *"Only careless people fall for scams."* Scammers are professionals, and anyone can be caught on a bad day.
- *"If they know my name and address, they must be real."* That information is easy to buy ([Get yourself off data broker sites](../practices/privacy-and-footprint/priv-data-broker-opt-out.md)).
- *"Telling my family would be embarrassing."* Telling family is how you stop it, and how you protect them too.

## Check for understanding (5 min)

1. Someone says they're your grandchild and needs money for bail, and asks you to keep it secret. What do you do?
2. A pop-up says your computer is infected and to call a number. What do you do?
3. Who can you call if you think you've lost money to a scam? (Your bank first, then the **National Elder Fraud Hotline, 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311)**.)

## Leave with one action

Everyone writes their family code word idea on a card to take home (not to share with the room), plus the Elder Fraud Hotline number.
